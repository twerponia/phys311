Student-Friendly Summary: Taylor 9.1–9.2 and 10.1–10.3

9.1 – Acceleration Without Rotation

Newton’s laws are strictly valid only in inertial frames (frames that are not accelerating or rotating). When we work in an accelerating frame, we can still use Newton’s second law if we add an extra “inertial” or “fictitious” force.

In an inertial frame $S_0$:

$m \ddot{\mathbf{r}}_0 = \mathbf{F}$

In an accelerating frame $S$ with acceleration $\mathbf{A}$:

$m \ddot{\mathbf{r}} = \mathbf{F} - m \mathbf{A}$

So we define the inertial force:

$\mathbf{F}_{\text{inertial}} = - m \mathbf{A}$

As Taylor writes, “This means we can continue to use Newton's second law in the noninertial frame $S$ provided we agree that in the noninertial frame we must add an extra force-like term, often called the inertial force: $F_{\text{inertial}} = -mA$.”

Pendulum in an Accelerating Car

Consider a pendulum of length $L$ in a car accelerating horizontally with acceleration $A$. In the car’s frame, gravity $\mathbf{g}$ and the inertial force $-m\mathbf{A}$ combine into an effective gravity:

$\mathbf{g}_{\text{eff}} = \mathbf{g} - \mathbf{A}$

The equilibrium angle $\theta_{\text{eq}}$ (measured from the vertical) satisfies:

$\theta_{\text{eq}} = \arctan\left(\dfrac{A}{g}\right)$

For small oscillations about this equilibrium, the pendulum behaves like an ordinary pendulum with $g$ replaced by $|\mathbf{g}_{\text{eff}}| = \sqrt{g^2 + A^2}$, so the angular frequency is:

$\omega = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sqrt{g^2 + A^2}}{L}}$

Taylor notes that “the pendulum's equation of motion in the accelerating frame of the car is exactly the same as in an inertial frame, except that $g$ has been replaced by an effective $g_{\text{eff}} = g - A$.”

9.2 – The Tides

Basic Idea

The moon’s gravity does not just pull on the oceans; it pulls on the entire Earth. The Earth (including its oceans) has a small acceleration $\mathbf{A}$ toward the moon. The tidal force at a point on Earth is the difference between:

  • Actual gravitational force of the moon at that point
  • Gravitational force the moon would exert at Earth’s center

Taylor writes: “the tidal force $F_{\text{tid}}$ … is the difference between the actual force of the moon on $m$ and the corresponding force if $m$ were at the center of the earth.”

Near Side and Far Side Bulges

  • Near side (facing the moon): moon’s pull is slightly stronger than at Earth’s center → water bulges toward the moon.
  • Far side (away from the moon): moon’s pull is slightly weaker → water effectively bulges away from the moon.

As Earth rotates, a point on the surface passes through both bulges → two high tides per day.

Equipotential Surface and Tide Height

The ocean surface is an equipotential surface, meaning the total potential energy (gravity of Earth + tidal potential) is constant along the surface.

Let $h$ be the height difference between high and low tide. Taylor shows:

$m g h = U_{\text{tid}}(Q) - U_{\text{tid}}(P)$

From his approximations, the tidal height due to the moon alone is:

$h_{\text{moon}} \approx \dfrac{3 M_m R_e}{2 M_e d_0}$

Numerically:

  • Moon alone: $h \approx 54\ \text{cm}$
  • Sun alone: $h \approx 25\ \text{cm}$
  • Spring tides (sun and moon aligned): $h \approx 79\ \text{cm}$
  • Neap tides (sun and moon at right angle): $h \approx 29\ \text{cm}$

Taylor concludes: “we would predict large tides (known as spring tides) with $h$ given by the sum of (9.19) and (9.20)… On the other hand, if the sun, earth, and moon form a right angle, then the two tidal effects will cancel and we would predict smaller tides (known as neap tides).”

10.1 – Properties of the Center of Mass

Center of Mass (CM)

For $N$ particles with masses $m_a$ and positions $\mathbf{r}_a$:

$\mathbf{R} = \dfrac{1}{M} \sum_{a=1}^{N} m_a \mathbf{r}_a,\quad M = \sum_{a=1}^{N} m_a$

Total Momentum

Total momentum:

$\mathbf{P} = \sum_a m_a \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a = M \dot{\mathbf{R}}$

So the CM moves as if all mass were concentrated at a single particle of mass $M$. Taylor states: “the CM moves just as if it were a single particle of mass $M$ subject to the net external force on the system.”

Angular Momentum Decomposition

Write each position as:

$\mathbf{r}_a = \mathbf{R} + \mathbf{r}'_a$

Then the total angular momentum about some origin $O$ is:

$\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{R} \times \mathbf{P} + \sum_a \mathbf{r}'_a \times m_a \dot{\mathbf{r}}'_a$

Interpretation:

  • First term: angular momentum of CM motion (orbital part).
  • Second term: angular momentum of motion relative to CM (spin part).

Kinetic Energy Decomposition

Total kinetic energy:

$T = \dfrac{1}{2} \sum_a m_a \dot{\mathbf{r}}_a^2$

Using $\mathbf{r}_a = \mathbf{R} + \mathbf{r}'_a$ and $\sum_a m_a \mathbf{r}'_a = 0$:

$T = \dfrac{1}{2} M \dot{\mathbf{R}}^2 + \dfrac{1}{2} \sum_a m_a \dot{\mathbf{r}}'^2_a$

So:

  • First term: translational kinetic energy of CM.
  • Second term: rotational kinetic energy about CM.

Taylor summarizes: “For a rigid body, the only possible motion relative to the CM is rotation. Thus we can rephrase this result to say that $T = T(\text{motion of CM}) + T(\text{rotation about CM}).”

10.2 – Rotation About a Fixed Axis

Moment of Inertia and Angular Momentum

Consider a rigid body rotating about the $z$-axis with angular velocity $\omega$. For each mass element $m_a$ at distance $p_a$ from the $z$-axis:

$L_z = \sum_a m_a p_a^2 \omega = I_{zz} \omega$

where:

$I_{zz} = \sum_a m_a p_a^2 = \sum_a m_a (x_a^2 + y_a^2)$

Products of Inertia and Misalignment

The $x$ and $y$ components of $\mathbf{L}$ are:

$L_x = I_{xz} \omega,\quad L_y = I_{yz} \omega$

with:

$I_{xz} = -\sum_a m_a x_a z_a,\quad I_{yz} = -\sum_a m_a y_a z_a$

If $I_{xz}$ and $I_{yz}$ are not zero, then $\mathbf{L}$ is not parallel to $\boldsymbol{\omega}$. Taylor emphasizes: “the angular momentum may not be in the same direction as the angular velocity, and the relation $L = I \omega$ that you may have learned in introductory physics is generally not true!”

Symmetry (e.g., reflection symmetry or axial symmetry) often makes these products of inertia vanish, so that $\mathbf{L}$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ align.

10.3 – Rotation About Any Axis; The Inertia Tensor

General Angular Velocity

Let the angular velocity be:

$\boldsymbol{\omega} = (\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z)$

The angular momentum components are:

$L_x = I_{xx} \omega_x + I_{xy} \omega_y + I_{xz} \omega_z$

$L_y = I_{yx} \omega_x + I_{yy} \omega_y + I_{yz} \omega_z$

$L_z = I_{zx} \omega_x + I_{zy} \omega_y + I_{zz} \omega_z$

Inertia Tensor

We collect these into the inertia tensor $I$:

$I = \begin{pmatrix} I_{xx} & I_{xy} & I_{xz} \\ I_{yx} & I_{yy} & I_{yz} \\ I_{zx} & I_{zy} & I_{zz} \end{pmatrix}$

Then:

$\mathbf{L} = I \boldsymbol{\omega}$

Taylor writes: “Equation (10.36) can be rewritten in matrix form… which is called the moment of inertia tensor or just inertia tensor.”

Principal Axes (Conceptual Preview)

There exist special directions (principal axes) where the inertia tensor becomes diagonal and $\mathbf{L}$ is parallel to $\boldsymbol{\omega}$. These axes are extremely useful for understanding the motion of rotating bodies (gyroscopes, tops, etc.).
